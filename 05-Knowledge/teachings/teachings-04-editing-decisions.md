# Teachings: Editing decisions

The goal drives every edit: every element must help the viewer understand or feel; show, don't tell.

Part of the [[05-Knowledge/teachings/teachings-archive|Teachings archive]]. From his prep voice notes (2026-10-16 to 2026-10-21, [[04-Projects/called-to-edit-webinar-teachings|prep record]]); what he added live on 2026-10-23 is marked **Live** with the time in the recording.

## The decisions an editor makes and what should drive them

**The goal drives every decision.** Every video has a goal; know it.
- A business video's goal: *"convert viewers into clients."*
- You measure your result by whether the video achieved the reason the client posted it.
- **Views vs sales:** a fancy video can get lots of views and no sales. A simple, clear video for the target audience can bring lots of sales. *"Which is more beneficial for the client?"*
- If the client's goal is views, then hit that goal instead.
- Ask: what is my client trying to achieve? What is their target audience looking for? What's the best thing this video presents? Build the edit around that.

**Every element needs a function.** *"Every cut, every animation that you do needs to have a reason for it to be in the video."* Each one must do one of two things:
1. **help the viewer understand** the knowledge in the video, or
2. **help the viewer feel an emotion.**
- Sad music used to carry the emotion of a story works. Sad music *"plastered for no reason"* doesn't.
- A transition has to play a role in the story.
- Random B-roll *"just to make it look like it is being edited"* is wrong. B-roll is there to help the audience see what's being said.

**Show, don't tell.** *"Whenever the client is saying something and you can show that thing, show it."*
- For clients: whatever he explains, Samuel visualises, with motion graphics and explainers.
- **Real beats fake:** if he's talking about a car, show a real car, not a car icon. *"A real thing always beats a fake thing."*
- Talking about YouTube views? Show YouTube views. A meeting? Show B-roll of that meeting if you can, or visualise it another way.
- Some of his videos are about **80% showing what the client says** rather than the client's face, depending on the video type.

**How viewers take in a video**
- **What they hear:** the speaker's voice helps them follow; sound effects carry feelings and emotion.
- **What they see:** visuals must match what they hear. A car sound needs something car-related on screen.
- *He said "three major ways a viewer digests a video" and named hearing (voice, sound effects) and seeing. Check what the third is.*

**Niche and platform shape the choices**
- Master one niche and you learn *"the ingredients"* that make its videos get results for the client.
- Platform matters: TikTok is fast; Instagram and YouTube editing each differ. Hold the viewer's attention.

**Why this makes you worth more:** *"A client will prefer an editor that can do videos that produce more results than a video editor that is doing flashy stuff and the video is not performing."* That's what tells you what to add or cut.

**Teaching:** edit for the client's goal, not to look edited. Every cut, effect, sound and B-roll must help the viewer understand or feel something. Show, don't tell.

**Examples:** visualising his clients' explanations with motion graphics · real car vs car icon · videos 80% visuals, not face · sad music with a purpose vs plastered.

**In his words:**
- *"Every cut, every animation that you do needs to have a reason for it to be in the video."*
- *"A real thing always beats a fake thing."*
- *"Show and not tell."*
- *"Views that he can't use for anything, or the sales?"*

## Community calls

What he taught on Called to Edit Community calls. Times are from the start of each call's recording; each call's note is listed in the [[05-Knowledge/teachings/teachings-archive|archive]].

### Call 2026-11-04: don't promise views you haven't delivered before
- A member asked: if your offer promises views and the video you edit doesn't bring them, does that make you less competent? His answer: no [0:57:00].
- **Call 2026-11-04 [0:57:22]** Stop pitching your skill and pitch the result, *"but for you to get result you have to learn more than video editing. You have to understand the psychology of content."*
- **Call 2026-11-04 [0:57:45]** *"Because a video is well edited does not mean it will bring more views."* You also need pacing (heard as "paste" in the transcript), the hook, content strategy, scriptwriting and storytelling [0:58:00–0:58:22].
- **Call 2026-11-04 [0:58:27]** *"The skill of video editing is bigger than just knowing how to cut and add flashy transitions."* *"You are more of a content partner than a video editor."*
- **Call 2026-11-04 [0:58:41]** If you can't yet make a video convert that much, *"don't promise a client views yet,"* not until your edits have brought that result before. [0:59:08] *"You have to be sure that you can do it, and you have to have done it before for someone else. Don't promise something that you are not 100% sure."*

### Call 2026-11-06: the basics matter more than the flashy stuff
- **His story** [0:01:44–0:05:01]: editing for more than five years, he joined *"a big agency, they work with billionaires"*. He already knew *"crazy motion graphics with DaVinci Resolve."* On his first edit for them, a 10-minute long-form video, the creative director put **80 corrections** [0:02:38]. *"Why am I getting corrections like a beginner?"* The answer: he was focused on flashy stuff and *"missing the basics."*
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:04:01]** *"What really matters in editing is not the visuals that you see, those fancy stuff, but the minor things that people don't see."* Examples [0:04:26]: storytelling, pacing, composition, your cuts, sound design, typography.
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:04:48]** *"Calm down with the flashy stuff. Funny enough, those flashy stuff, the big boys don't even want it. They want the basic simple stuff."*
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:36:33]** *"Little basic things can make a simple video look good or bad."*
- The basics he listed [0:16:45–0:17:21]: the audience, storytelling, cutting, pacing, composition, sound and sound effects, and how you export. They apply in any software, and to motion graphics, talking heads and vlogs alike.

### Call 2026-11-06: edit for the audience, not for yourself
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:05:38]** *"You are editing for an audience. Not for yourself, not for your clients, but for an audience."* The best result comes when the audience understands the video, and it tells the story or makes them feel something.
- **Four questions to ask** [0:06:05–0:07:01]: what kind of videos am I editing (know your niche)? Who is watching? Where will they watch: TikTok, Instagram or YouTube? What should they feel, and what should they do (buy the course, the program)?
- Platform shapes the style: *"even though TikTok and Instagram look the same, it's still not the same audience"* [0:06:35].
- The client still has the final say [0:07:08], *"but more importantly, try and edit for your audience."*
- Beginners skip the audience and the storytelling and go straight to cutting [0:08:50].

### Call 2026-11-06: 80% of video editing is removing the fluff
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:09:25]** *"80% of video editing is removing the fluff."* Fluff: filler words, things that don't matter, things that don't help the audience. *"Your responsibility as the video editor is to make sure that you leave only the valuable stuff."*
- Why [0:10:25]: it helps retention and understanding, and you're not wasting anyone's time. *"Once a viewer starts seeing too much fluff, they won't watch the video to the end."*
- Even a client's own script has fluff [0:10:56]. **Call 2026-11-06 [0:11:03]** *"You have to learn to remove things more than add things."*

### Call 2026-11-06: pacing is rhythm, and fast isn't always better
- Pacing is the rhythm of a video [0:11:21]: how dense the information, the motion graphics and the cuts are.
- **The music picture** [0:11:31–0:12:47]: the faster the beat, the more people dance; *"someone that is dancing too much in music does not really pay attention to the lyrics."* A slower song makes people listen to the words. Same in editing.
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:12:47]** Fast pace may make people watch through the video, *"but does not mean they will understand the video."* The audience and the purpose of the video set the pace.
- Pace follows the story [0:13:33]: the hook usually fast, slower as the payoff comes in.
- **His proof, on screen** [0:50:00–0:51:48]: his webinar ad opens slow on the line about his first USD 20,000 in video editing (figure as heard). *"I want that information to soak in. Imagine if I was doing a fast pace cut at the intro, it wouldn't be able to pass that feeling."* Then the cuts got faster as the video went on. **Call 2026-11-06 [0:51:42]** *"The more frequent your B-rolls or animations are, that is what is called pacing."*
- Gaps depend on the audience too [0:26:49]: he cuts with no gaps for his audience; for an older audience or YouTube, leave little gaps.

### Call 2026-11-06: composition: one focus per frame
- Composition is how things are placed in the video [0:14:04]: the framing of the speaker, the typography, the motion graphics; grid lines, the rule of thirds (heard as "rule of thoughts"), safe zones on Instagram.
- **Frame the speaker first** [0:36:42–0:37:12]: before or after cutting, centre the subject with the framing tools, zooming in a little to give room to move them.
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:39:53]** *"Every video that you have has a focal point."* In a talking head it's the face: the viewer looks at the eyes. Put motion graphics far down and they must watch two places at once. **Call 2026-11-06 [0:40:26]** *"The further the focal points, the more difficult it is. You as the video editor need to guide the audience to where they should focus."*
- **In a talking head with animations** [0:42:16]: the face, the typography and the motion graphics. Keep the captions close to the face, *"because the audience uses that to read what the person is saying."*
- **Keep them on one line of focus** [0:44:05], horizontal, vertical or diagonal, so the eye moves along it. Or move the speaker into a corner by the rule of thirds and use the space beside him.
- Animating to the scene [0:41:43]: things that sit on the table in the shot, even 3D typography on the table. Those are a second focus.
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:43:50]** *"What I see a lot of editors do is that they put things on screen without a proper focal point."* [0:45:18] *"We think putting things on screen is just because we want to put things on screen. You don't just do that."*

### Call 2026-11-06: sound design passes the feeling
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:14:59]** *"Sound design is very, very powerful. A video badly edited but with good sound design, it still has this feeling."* [0:15:07] *"Sound design is where you pass the feeling of a video."* Edit only the visuals and the audience can't feel the emotion.
- Sound covers the voice, the music and the sound effects [0:15:21–0:16:01]. If the voice isn't clear, viewers won't understand and will scroll past; *"it's your job as the video editor to make sure that the voice is clear enough, irrespective of the mic the person uses."* Then music, ducking, and effects like pops, impacts, risers and ambience.

### Call 2026-11-06: section your video and treat each section differently
- **On screen** [0:52:04–0:54:58]: a client's 8-minute long-form video on his timeline, every section a different clip colour: hook, sections one to five, a mid-video call to action, sections six to eight, outro. **Every section had different music**; he never used the same music in every section.
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:53:07]** *"I don't look at the video as one block of edits. I look at it as different sections."*
- Short form has sections too [0:54:18]: the hook (intro), the setup or body, the payoff, the ending (often the call to action). Each is edited differently.
- **Call 2026-11-06 [0:54:45]** *"Stop treating your video as one whole video at once, because the pacing differs depending on what the person is saying in every section."*

### Call 2026-11-06: storytelling for the editor
- A member asked how to go deeper into storytelling [1:05:43]. It's in the class curriculum; his summary [1:06:15]: storytelling *"is how you carry the viewer through the feelings and the understanding of the video."*
- **Rearranging is storytelling** [1:06:43]: if something important is said inside a talking head, cutting it and bringing it closer to the intro is storytelling.
- **The arc** [1:07:24]: curiosity, context, conflict, pivot, resolution.
- **Curiosity in the edit** [1:07:48–1:08:23]: the hook says *"there are five ways to make NGN 5 million as a video editor."* Write the five out on screen and you've given it away; write them and blur them, and you've made curiosity. Then context (*"five years ago I started video editing"*), conflict (*"a lot of video editors are losing clients because of these reasons"*), then the meat: step one, two, three.
- **When the client wrote the script** [1:09:18]: you're not in control of the story, but knowing storytelling tells you where to speed the pacing up and where to slow it down *"so that the viewer can absorb the information."* Sound design, zoom in and zoom out, and typography all add to it. **Call 2026-11-06 [1:09:53]** *"Every section of the video should have its own feeling."*
