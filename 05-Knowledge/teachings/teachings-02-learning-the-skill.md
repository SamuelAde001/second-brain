# Teachings: Learning the skill

What to learn first, where, how (learning list, recreations), when you're ready to charge, working free.

Part of the [[05-Knowledge/teachings/teachings-archive|Teachings archive]]. From his prep voice notes (2026-10-16 to 2026-10-21, [[04-Projects/called-to-edit-webinar-teachings|prep record]]); what he added live on 2026-10-23 is marked **Live** with the time in the recording.

## What to learn first when you don't know where to start

**The core idea:** *"You don't choose what to learn. What you learn is chosen for you"* by a few factors. You work backwards from them and *"eliminate what you don't need till you finally know what you need to learn."*

**The factors that choose what you learn**
1. **Who you want to work for, and what kind of videos.** Be very specific.
   - On YouTube: vlogs, business talking heads, reaction, gaming, podcast, storytelling, cinematic.
   - On Instagram: reaction, talking head, storytelling.
   - Adverts are a category of their own.
   - Platforms edit differently: *"YouTube talking head and Instagram talking head. They are not the same thing."* TikTok is different again, and so is LinkedIn. Short form vs long form.
2. **Who can actually pay you.** Business videos pay more than entertainment. Paying niches he named: AI and SaaS (his), real estate (*"They have money"*), logistics, solar companies. *"You need to niche into something first before you even pick your skill."*
3. **What you have: your hardware.** Phone or laptop, and how strong it is.
4. **What you have: your software.** CapCut can't do 3D motion graphics, *"so why learn the skill?"*

**Each kind of video needs different skills**
- Real estate: a lot of tracking and animation.
- Business talking heads: no tracking or animation needed.
- Some storytelling videos: visual effects.
- Adverts: other skills, some need visual effects.
- Some videos don't even need sound effects.

**Where phone editors win and lose**
- Lose: long-form YouTube, motion graphics, heavy animation. *"You can't compete with a computer video editor."* Many phone editors add animations because everyone else does.
- Win: simple short form, **event recaps**, talking heads cut into shorts that just need captions, simple social-media-style videos with captions, overlays and screenshots. *"Most of the mobile video editors I see succeeding very well are those ones that do event recaps and simple social media styled videos."*
- *"Focus on where your power can handle."*

**Worked example: a phone editor doing podcast recaps**
- Learn: cutting, sound design, typography and captions.
- Skip: motion graphics, *"crazy storytelling"*.

**Then define yourself.** Don't just say "I'm a video editor": there are many (movie, TikTok, visual effects, podcast). His line: *"I am a long-form video editor that edits business videos for YouTubers."* Specialising is what makes you stand out. Every niche chosen this way has an audience. The next step after learning is finding those people.

**The foundations every paid edit needs:** cutting, pacing, audio, captions.

**Software vs skill: learn both, and they're different**
- **Master the software first**, the one you'll actually use. For CapCut, sit through the long 4–5 hour tutorials. *"This is a part that you can't skip."* He mastered DaVinci Resolve: *"There's nothing you can tell me about DaVinci Resolve that I don't know about."*
- **The skill** is what achieves the kind of video you chose: the art of cutting, pacing, sound.
- The mistake: learning the software and thinking that's editing, without knowing how to run the skill.
- Your tool stack grows as you go, e.g. CapCut plus Claude as an AI tool.

**AI as part of what you learn**
- AI is a tool in your stack, depending on the videos you edit.
- It can't do cinematic, heavily animated videos yet. It can handle some simple talking heads, and on a laptop it can cut your videos for you.
- He uses Claude (Claude Code) in his own workflow to edit parts of his videos.
- A friend works with a client whose short forms are all edited by AI.
- **AI as a pitch:** *"I use AI to enhance your videos faster so that you can put out content faster."* Some creators and business owners *"need speed more than even fancy stuff."*
- Use AI tools if you can afford them, then pitch them, and **charge the client for the cost of the AI tools** you use. *(Checked 2026-10-19: he meant "AI cost", not "course".)*

**Rule for beginners:** *"Don't learn what you are not going to be able to pitch out to your client."* Everything looks exciting. Motion graphics is fine to learn if it's your target audience, everything checks out and your tools can handle it.

**Then where to learn is simple: YouTube.** It has everything. The problem is trying to learn everything (more in 1.2).

**Teaching:** don't start with the skill. Start with who you serve, what they pay for and what your tools can do. Cut everything else, then learn the software and the few skills that are left.

**Examples:** he wanted to learn everything (a flip animation he saw) and it was overwhelming, until he chose YouTube long-form business talking heads · he loved motion graphics but his laptop couldn't handle it, so he dropped it · he mastered Resolve · he uses Claude in his edits · a friend's client gets all short forms edited by AI · phone editors winning on event recaps.

**In his words:**
- *"You don't choose what to learn. What you learn is chosen for you."*
- *"You need to niche into something first before you even pick your skill."*
- *"Don't just say I'm a video editor."*
- *"Focus on where your power can handle."*
- *"Don't learn what you are not going to be able to pitch out to your client."*

## Where to learn: tutorials

**Where: YouTube, for the skill.** *"I didn't learn from any other place than YouTube."* *"There's nothing you want to learn that you won't learn there."* He never paid for a skills course.

**The problem isn't the source, it's the search**
- People think one video can teach them all of video editing. *"No, that's wrong. Video editing is broad."*
- What you search for comes from 1.1: who you serve, what kind of video, who pays more, your hardware, your software.
- Example: he wanted typography for his videos, so he searched "typography tutorial", learned it and implemented it.

**Build a learning list (the method)**
1. Name your video type, e.g. talking-head business videos for Instagram creators.
2. Ask any AI tool: *"What are the skills I need to learn to edit talking head videos for business creators on Instagram?"*
3. It returns a list, e.g. cutting, pacing, captions, a little typography, overlays and outlines, B-roll, sound design, music.
4. Learn them **one by one**: watch one tutorial for one skill.
5. **Implement it straight away** on one practice video: a full video from YouTube or anywhere, or *record yourself on your phone*. Learn cutting → cut your own video. Learn typography → add it to that same video. Then sound design, then a little motion graphics, all on the same video.
- *"Don't learn without implementation."*

**Learn as you work**
- Mid-edit and don't know a transition? Search YouTube for it, or describe the transition you saw to an AI and ask how to do it. Learn it, then implement it.
- With clients: when a client asks "can you do this?", he says yes, then learns it while doing that project.
- *"I actually learned on the go more than just watching tutorials."*
- *"You can't learn video editing without actually being in the videos, practicing it."*

**No single teacher:** he recommends no specific creator. He learned from many, each for what he needed at that moment, never one creator from beginning to end.

**Make tutorials your entertainment:** there was a time he opened YouTube and watched tutorials for fun. *"Let the tutorials become your entertainment."*

**What's a waste of time:** learning something you don't know you'll implement. Watching one whole full tutorial instead of learning skill by skill from your list.

**Free vs paid**
- *"Everything you can pay for in a course when it comes to the skill is on YouTube."* Don't pay for skills.
- What YouTube hardly teaches is **the business**: talking with clients, packaging yourself, presenting yourself, *"everything I'm teaching here"*. YouTube teaches *"fancy stuff that you don't need"*.
- He paid for a community to learn the business. He learned the business side from marketers, sales people and people who run ads, not from video editors.
- *"If you don't know what you want to learn, that's when you end up paying for courses. And most of the time, you will not implement them."*

**AI tools:** once you find AI tools that help your editing, learn each one the same way, individually. Describing what you saw to an AI is itself a way to learn.

**Teaching:** YouTube has every skill. Make a learning list for your kind of video, learn one skill at a time and use it on a real video straight away. Pay only to learn the business.

**Examples:** typography search · saying yes to a client, then learning it on the job · watching tutorials for fun · paying for a community for the business side, taught by marketers and sales people.

**In his words:**
- *"Video editing is broad."*
- *"Don't learn without implementation."*
- *"Let the tutorials become your entertainment."*
- *"Everything you can pay for in a course when it comes to the skill is on YouTube."*
- *"If you don't know what you want to learn, that's when you end up paying for courses."*

## How to learn: recreate videos you like instead of only watching

**Recreating videos in your niche is one of the fastest ways to learn.** It gets easier once you know what kind of videos you edit (1.1), because you know where to look. *"One of the things I did a lot was recreate what people have already edited."*

**How (his podcast example)**
1. Pick your niche, e.g. podcast videos.
2. Find an edit you love, e.g. someone's short form cut from a podcast.
3. Find the raw podcast on YouTube and download it.
4. Mimic the same edit.
5. When you hit something you can't do (an animation, a visual, a 3D effect, the sound design), search YouTube for how to do that one thing, learn it, then put it in. *"There's a YouTube tutorial for everything you want to do. I promise you."*

**Where to get raw footage**
- *"YouTube is a mine of raw videos."* Many people post unedited videos.
- **Don't use the biggest creators:** their videos are already over-edited. Look for raw videos similar to your niche.
- If you can't find the raw, use another kind of video.
- Download with one of the many download tools. **He plans to show them how to download YouTube videos live.** *Which tool: not said yet.*

**Share it:** put the original and yours side by side on social media: *"I just recreated this edit. What do you guys think? Did I get it right?"* Lots of editors post recreations.

**Practice, not portfolio:** *"a recreated video might not really be going into your portfolio... it's mostly for practice."*

**Why it works**
- You're implementing what someone already did professionally, *"so that you can one day become like that person."*
- It sharpens your skills and challenges you. You've never done it before and can't ask the editor who did it, so you have to work out how.
- He did it with motion graphics and styles he admired.

**Teaching:** pick an edit you love in your niche, find raw footage and copy it shot for shot, learning each missing piece on YouTube as you hit it.

**Examples:** recreating podcast shorts from the raw podcast · recreating motion graphics and styles he admired.

**In his words:**
- *"There's a YouTube tutorial for everything you want to do. I promise you."*
- *"YouTube is a mine of raw videos."*
- *"Don't look for the biggest creators. Those guys have already over-edited their videos."*

## How long until you're good enough to charge

**The answer: no fixed time, a finished-video test.** *"When you can finish a full video from beginning to the end, you're already good enough to charge."* Specifically, your target type of video, start to finish. *"There's no real time. It is more of your skill level."*

**His standard: three full videos in your niche, start to finish** (*"let me not say one, but let's say... three videos in your niche"*). Then you're good enough to charge.

**The path from practice to paid**
1. **Practice videos:** your own, e.g. two or three talking heads of yourself.
2. **Move quickly to real people's videos, for free.** *"As soon as possible, transition from practicing to real-world projects."* Who:
   - a friend who owns a business: *"Record a video for your business, let me help you edit it so you can post it"*
   - an event you go to: capture it, edit it, show them so they can use it
   - small businesses near you without the budget for a video editor
   - small creators, or a friend making content in the style you want to edit
   These people can't pay great editors but can let you learn on their videos.
3. **Ask for a token:** if they're enjoying it, tell them you'd love to be paid a token for it.
4. **Charge.**

**Free and paid at the same time:** offer free help, but still put your work out there as a paid service. If someone sees your practice videos and asks *"How much do you charge?"*, don't tell them *"I'm not ready."*

**Free work turns into paid work:** a time comes when the person has to start paying you. If they won't, you can leave for another client, and they won't want you to leave.

**Why people wait too long to feel ready**
- They never start and finish a whole project.
- They learn *"nuggets"* and specific styles, not full projects, so they don't know how to put it together on a real video.
- Finish one, two, three full projects and you build the confidence to try a real person's video.

**Teaching:** you're ready to charge when you've finished three full videos in your niche. Get onto real people's videos fast, free at first, then ask for a token.

**Examples:** his mentor: free videos at first, then he started paying as Samuel's skill improved (0.1 has the NGN 25,000 a month) · a friend's business video · editing an event you attended.

**In his words:**
- *"When you can finish a full video from beginning to the end, you're already good enough to charge."*
- *"There's no real time."*
- *"As soon as possible, transition from practicing to real-world projects."*
- *"A lot of people are learning nuggets."*

## Working for free or low pay as a learning mechanism

**Real projects are the best and fastest way to learn.** *"My number one way to learn is working on real projects that have real consequences and are for real people."*

**Why real projects teach faster: pressure**
- If a friend or client will post it on their Instagram, you try your best not to make mistakes, so it converts and gets views.
- Your own practice video has no deadline. You can spend a whole month on one.
- When you know someone is posting tomorrow, it keeps you up late.
- To get real projects as a beginner, offer your service free, to people who can manage you as a beginner, where excellence isn't critical yet.

**The money ladder**
1. **Free:** the best way to start.
2. **Low pay**, once you're more confident and have done real work, *"even if it is 5K, 10K"* (NGN). It still lets you get better, and it pays for what learning costs: **data, electricity**. *"Whether you're working for free or not, you still need to get data."*
3. Don't stay free too long. *"We spend too much time learning and working for free. And that's why we don't have the money to afford the little things that we need."*
4. Reality check: you hear editors say *"I'm earning 500,000 naira, I'm earning 1 million."* *"You are not there yet. You are still in the learning stage."* At that stage you need enough money to fund your skill.

**How long is too long:** after **four or five free projects** for someone, start moving to paid, unless you have an agreement.

**Set the scope up front: a time frame or a number of videos**
- Not *"free for a year"*.
- The ask: *"Sir, please, can I edit your videos for one month for free? I'm trying to build my portfolio and I'm looking for more work... I would love to partner with you."* Or *"let me just do five videos for you, for free."*
- **Don't say you're learning:** say you're building your portfolio.
- His example: an editor once worked for him free for a whole month; after that he started paying them.

**The money conversation: you don't have to raise it.** With a scope set, when it ends the person knows either you leave or they pay you. If you did a good job, they know they may not find someone as good, so *"he will want to pay you to stay."* Then you renegotiate a new deal. (Negotiating contracts and batched deals comes later: 6.x, 9.2.)

**What free work gives you besides practice**
- **Testimonials and client results.** People who see the work won't know it was free: *"maybe this person has paid you huge."*
- **A portfolio** with real things to show.
- **Years of experience.** *"I used to say I have five years of experience. Out of that five years, three years... was majorly me working for free. At most, I was earning 25,000 naira. But no one knows that."*
- **Confidence.**
- **Referrals.**
- **Sharper skills.**

**Mindset:** *"You have to learn to humble yourself and work for free."* Most people think they need to get paid; you want to get better.

**Teaching:** work free on real projects for the pressure and the proof, with a set scope (one month or five videos). Move to low pay fast enough to fund your data and power.

**Examples:** his three years free or on at most NGN 25,000 a month, counted in his five years · an editor who worked free for him for a month, then got paid · staying up late for a friend's post.

**In his words:**
- *"Real projects that have real consequences and are for real people."*
- *"Whether you're working for free or not, you still need to get data."*
- *"You are not there yet. You are still in the learning stage."*
- *"Don't tell the person you're learning. Tell them you're building your portfolio."*
- *"You have to learn to humble yourself and work for free."*

## Live at the webinar, 2026-10-23

What he said live that the prep didn't have, or said better. Times are from the start of the OBS recording.

**The order is backwards**
- **Live [0:08:21]** *"It's easy to find tutorials. The hardest part is moving from practicing and tutorials to getting paid."*
- **Live [1:15:59]** He drew it on the board: most people learn the skill, then go looking for someone to pay for it. *"The opposite is meant to be the way."* Start from the dream client and the kind of video, then ask what skills those videos need. That's your learning list.
- **Live [0:35:18]** **Be an "AI video editor".** Not someone who edits AI videos: an editor who uses AI to edit for people. They still pay you, because it's still a service. It also helps editors without a strong computer.

**Live demo: the learning list with AI** [1:17:40]
- He typed into Claude: *"I want to be able to learn how to edit finance videos for my clients. What skills in video editing do I need to master?"* (ChatGPT gives the same; a fast model is fine.)
- Finance came back as: the fundamentals (cutting, pacing, story structure, audio, colour correction), plus motion graphics for animated charts, lower thirds, screen recordings and typography.
- Podcast came back different: multicam, rhythm and trimming, a lot of audio work, and turning long-form episodes into short clips. *"Do you see motion graphics in podcast? Do you see flashy stuff in podcast?"*
- Building your list is a workbook task. *"Every video has its own skills."*

**Live demo: searching YouTube** [1:27:14]
- Don't search "how to edit". Broad tutorials can't be specific. Search the exact skill on your list, e.g. *"how to do multicam editing in DaVinci Resolve"* or in CapCut, and tick it off.
- *"I can learn CapCut and still not know how to edit videos."* The app is the tool; the skill is separate.
- *"YouTube is your baby."* He can't teach every kind of editing because he focused on one: business talking-head long form on YouTube.

**Recreations, shown live** [1:33:37]: his old motion-graphics recreation, a *"cringe"* VFX practice on a video of himself, and a before-and-after of his mentor's video in a style he was copying.

**Practice to paid, new lines**
- **Live [1:37:23]** *"Who is guilty here? You want to create content for yourself, but because it's for yourself, you procrastinate. If it is for someone else, you will take it seriously."* Practice edits work the same way. That's why real projects teach faster.
- **Live [1:38:08]** He edited free for his mentor *"for almost one year"* (in the prep he put the unpaid stretch longer: see his story).
- **Live [1:39:09]** After one or two months free, ask for a token: NGN 10,000 or 20,000. A good editor is someone they won't want to lose.
- **Live [1:40:15]** Events: film it, edit it, show the owner: *"See the video I edited for your wedding, do you like it? If you like it, you can pay me."* Some will.
- **Live [1:40:32]** *"We are looking far when we have not mastered the small."* Start with small creators and small businesses around you.
- **Live [1:41:19]** *"Our mindset is: I'm not perfect enough for people to pay me. But how would you get perfect if you are not editing many videos?"*
- **Live [1:41:37]** Then low pay, NGN 5,000 a video. *"Don't worry too much about the money yet. Try and get as many reps as possible."*
- **Live [1:42:24]** **"People are not looking for skill. They are looking for experience."** (He made the room write it down.) Companies ask how many years you have. Experience is who you've worked with, and *"it doesn't matter whether you worked with them for free."*
- **Live [1:43:38]** A simpler ask: *"Sir, please, I want to edit your videos for free. Don't worry, you don't need to post it. Just give me your raw talking head, let me be editing it."* Then it goes in your portfolio: *"it has become your own experience."*
- **Live [1:44:45]** Free work gave him a referral that mattered: **his mentor sent him the client who paid him his first NGN 100,000.** *"I can never forget that client."*

**How long to learn** (from the Q&A, [3:11:48]): *"If you find yourself focusing on everything, you will find yourself spending one year to learn. But if I tell you to learn how to cut videos for podcasts and put subtitles, you will need just one week."* With a proper niche, one month, if you're serious.

**Be self-taught** (Q&A, [3:09:04]): break editing into small pieces (colour grading, typography) and each one is learnable on YouTube. *"I never bought one course. The only thing I ever paid for in my video editing journey was a community."* He read a 1,000-page DaVinci Resolve book on Fusion to master it. *"If you don't know how to learn things by yourself, you'll never become a master."* When a style isn't on YouTube, ask editors who do it; he once **paid an editor to teach him a style** [3:02:59].
