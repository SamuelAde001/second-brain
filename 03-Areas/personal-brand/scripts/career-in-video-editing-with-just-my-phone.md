## Script

One seated shot, relaxed, like he's talking to one person. Punch in on the lines in bold. B-roll is optional: a talk works without it.

{seated, straight to camera}
"So can you actually build a career in video editing with just your phone?

Yes, you can. But there's a right way of doing it.

You see, I didn't start editing with a phone. But I've mentored people who started editing on their phones, and I've seen a lot of phone editors actually succeed. And they succeed because of the way they present themselves. **Because the gadget you use for editing is not the limitation. The way you package and present yourself is.**

The problem I see is this. A lot of mobile video editors see someone like me doing crazy motion graphics and animations on my clients' videos, and they think they can do that too. On their phone. That's where it goes wrong. You're competing on the wrong ground. You have to position yourself based on what your phone can carry.

{punch in}
And please, when you're pitching to a client, don't go and tell them you're a mobile video editor. **That will entirely spoil your game.** Because the moment they hear that, they might just feel like you're not good enough. And the truth is, most of these clients don't really care what you used to edit. They care about the result.

{back out}
So let me give you examples. A lot of the mobile editors I see succeed today are doing event recaps, because there's a demand for them. Church events, gym sessions, even family meetings.

Or let's say I'm the founder of a brand and I gave a speech at an event. If an editor can cut that speech into simple short clips for my social media, do I care that they used a phone? No, right? You don't need complex animations at that level. You can compete there.

And for things like that, what you really need is to tell the story, the emotion of the event. You see, if you start adding animations and typography on a phone, it shows. But if you can tell the story with the clips themselves, the way you cut, the sound design, the way you slow things down to allow the emotion, then you can use your phone very well.

So instead of 'I'm a mobile video editor', present yourself as 'I can help you repackage your event into a memorable short clip.' They don't need to know what you used. They just need to see the work, and feel the emotion in it.

{punch in}
Now, I'll be honest with you. I started with a laptop, and that's because I was lucky enough to be a salary earner. I spent all my salary on my first laptop. And the truth is, if you want to make substantial money in video editing, the bigger money comes from a laptop.

{back out}
But you have to start from somewhere. I know a lot of editors that started with their phones, and today they've bought their first laptop.

So why don't we get good at the little thing our phone can do first, before we start thinking about the big long-form videos? Become the best at it. Package it, reach out to people. And over time, that money can buy you a better phone, and then your first laptop.

**Cut your coat according to your size, and work with what you have.**

I'll be sharing a lot more about this at my upcoming webinar, whether you're using a phone or not. Called to Edit, Friday, October 23rd, 7pm. Comment EDIT, or check the link in my bio."

## Open questions for Samuel

1. **Talking heads.** He said phone editors succeed with *"simple talking head videos"*, then *"I will even tell you to avoid talking head videos"* because animations and typography on a phone show. Does he mean avoid talking heads altogether, or only the ones that need animated text? The draft leaves the line out. The "it shows" paragraph covers the second reading.
2. **One real mentee.** The script has no number in it. One true example would make it land: how many of his mentees started on a phone, what kind of job the first one got paid for, how much, and how long until the laptop. Without one, it stays "I know a lot of editors".
