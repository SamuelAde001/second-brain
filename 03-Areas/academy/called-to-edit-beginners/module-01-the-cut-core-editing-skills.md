## 05. How Editing Software Actually Works
**Shot type:** Screen Record

*Reframed from a CapCut tour. This is the anatomy of every non-linear editor, demonstrated in CapCut.*

**1. Open**
- Every editing program on earth is the same four ideas wearing different paint. Learn the four and you can open anything

**2. Principle**
- **The timeline:** time runs left to right, the playhead is where you are
- **Tracks and layers:** anything higher covers anything lower; audio runs underneath
- **Non-destructive editing:** you never damage the original file, you're writing instructions
- **Source vs sequence:** your media pool vs the arrangement you're building
- The three operations that make up 90% of all editing: trim, split, move
- Why every editor has the same core tools with different names

**3. Demo**
- Create a project, set the sequence to 9:16
- Import footage, show where media lives vs where the timeline lives
- Timeline navigation: zoom, scrub, snap
- Split, delete, ripple vs leaving a gap — show both, name the difference
- Layering: drop one clip above another, show it cover the one below
- Keyboard shortcuts worth learning day one — and that every NLE has an equivalent set **[CHECK: current CapCut PC shortcut set]**
- Undo/redo, autosave, project files

**4. The Trap**
- Trying to learn the whole interface before making anything. You need about six controls to start

**5. Drill**
- Import the practice footage, put three clips in order, top and tail each one
- That's it. That's a sequence

## 06. Cutting Talking-Head Footage
**Shot type:** Screen Record

**1. Open**
- The cut is the edit. Everything else in this course is decoration on top of this skill

**2. Principle**
- Watch first, mark keepers — you're selecting, not just deleting
- Cutting for meaning: keep what advances the point, remove what repeats it
- **Filler and false starts:** ums, restarts, the two seconds before they actually begin
- **Breath cuts:** removing dead air changes the perceived energy of a person more than any effect will
- **Jump cuts:** invisible when the frame and the idea both continue; jarring when either breaks
- How tight is too tight — the edit that leaves the viewer no room to absorb anything
- The seconds test: does this moment earn the time it takes?

**3. Demo**
- Play a 90-second raw take start to finish so they hear the original
- Pass 1: remove false starts and dead air. Play the result
- Pass 2: remove repeated ideas and weak sentences. Play the result
- Pass 3: tighten the gaps between sentences. Play the result
- Show one cut you got wrong and fix it on camera

**4. The Trap**
- Cutting every single pause until it sounds like a machine gun
- Keeping a section because it was hard to film

**5. Drill**
- Take the 90-second practice take down to 45 seconds
- Watch it back once, then cut 5 more seconds

## 07. Pacing
**Shot type:** Screen Record

**1. Open**
- Pacing isn't how fast you cut. It's how fast new information arrives

**2. Principle**
- Rate of information vs rate of cuts — a slow shot with a new idea in it is fast
- Fast isn't automatically better: what speed costs you in emotion and comprehension
- Pacing serves the arc: build, hold, release
- Where to compress — setup, context, anything the viewer can infer
- Where to breathe — the payoff, the emotional beat, the punchline landing
- Cutting to the beat vs cutting to the meaning, and when to let music lead
- Variation across a full edit: an unchanging rhythm goes numb no matter how fast it is
- Diagnosing a flat edit: watch it muted, then watch it at 2x — both expose pacing problems instantly

**3. Demo**
- Cut the same 30-second section twice: aggressive, then relaxed
- Play both back to back, name what each one does emotionally
- Take a flat edit and fix it live by changing only shot durations — no new footage

**4. The Trap**
- Cutting fast to hide weak content. It doesn't work; it just makes weak content frantic
- Locking to the music beat so rigidly that meaning gets sliced in half

**5. Drill**
- Cut the same clip set twice — 20 seconds and 40 seconds
- Write one line on what the longer version gains

## 08. A-Roll / B-Roll Blending
**Shot type:** Screen Record

**1. Open**
- B-roll isn't filler you add at the end. It's a cut you make for a reason

**2. Principle**
- What A-roll and B-roll each do: one carries the argument, one carries the evidence and the energy
- Four good reasons to cut away: illustrate the point, hide an edit, control pace, inject energy
- The bad reason: because you have footage and it feels like a waste
- **Timing:** cut to the B-roll on the word, not two beats after it
- How long to hold — the shot that outstays its purpose kills momentum
- Using B-roll to cover jump cuts invisibly
- Keeping the A-roll audio running underneath so the argument never stops
- Cutting back to the face at the right moment — usually just before the next important idea

**3. Demo**
- Layer B-roll on the track above A-roll, show audio continuing underneath
- Place three cutaways: one to illustrate, one to hide a cut, one for energy — and narrate why each
- Show the same cutaway placed one second late, so they hear it feel wrong

**4. The Trap**
- Wall-to-wall B-roll with no face — the viewer loses the person and the trust
- Generic cutaways that illustrate nothing (stock hands typing over an unrelated point)

**5. Drill**
- Add exactly three cutaways to your Lesson 06 cut
- For each one, write the reason in one word: illustrate / hide / pace / energy

## 09. Building a Video From B-Roll Alone
**Shot type:** Screen Record

**1. Open**
- Take away the talking, and structure has to come from the images. This is where you find out if you can actually sequence

**2. Principle**
- Sequencing as grammar: wide establishes, medium develops, detail reveals
- How order creates meaning — the same three shots in a different order say something different
- Spotting visual interest in ordinary places: light, texture, movement, scale, contrast
- Shot variety and the repetition problem — two similar shots in a row read as one
- Letting music carry the structure without becoming a slideshow with a soundtrack
- Duration discipline: how long a silent shot holds before attention leaks
- Building a small arc with no words: setup, turn, resolve

**3. Demo**
- Lay out a clip set and build a 20-second piece live
- Rearrange the same shots into a second order, show how the meaning shifts
- Add music, adjust the cut points to the track, show what changes

**4. The Trap**
- The slideshow: every shot the same length, no arc, no reason for the order
- Choosing shots because they're pretty rather than because they say something

**5. Drill**
- Build a 20-second B-roll piece from the practice pack
- Then rebuild it in a different order and decide which one is better

## 10. Transitions
**Shot type:** Screen Record

**1. Open**
- The default transition is a cut, and it's the right answer about 95% of the time

**2. Principle**
- What a transition is actually for: continuity, change of time or place, energy
- Why the cut is invisible and why invisible is usually the goal
- **Match cuts** — the one transition that adds meaning instead of noise. Match on shape, motion, action or sound
- Motion transitions (whip pans, camera-driven moves): how they work, when they fit the content's energy
- Preset transitions and why most of them read as amateur — they announce the software
- Sound-led transitions: an audio cue makes a visual change feel intentional
- The test: remove the transition. Is the video worse? If no, it was decoration

**3. Demo**
- Build a match cut from scratch on real footage
- Build a whip pan from a camera move plus a cut, no preset
- Apply three CapCut preset transitions and talk honestly about how each reads
- Add a sound effect to a plain cut and show how much it does on its own

**4. The Trap**
- Transitioning between every single clip
- Using a transition to fix a cut that just doesn't work — the transition doesn't fix it, it highlights it

**5. Drill**
- Find one place in your edit that genuinely needs a transition and build it
- Remove every other transition in the project

## 11. Keyframing 101
**Shot type:** Screen Record

**1. Open**
- One mechanic. Learn it here and typography, motion graphics and effects all become the same skill

**2. Principle**
- A keyframe is a value pinned to a moment in time
- Two keyframes make movement; the software fills the gap
- The four properties you'll use constantly: position, scale, rotation, opacity
- **Easing** — why linear motion looks robotic, and what ease in/out does to the feel
- The subtle push-in: the single most useful keyframe move in short-form
- Motion should have a reason: direct attention, add energy, reveal information
- This exact concept exists in every editor and every motion tool — the panel changes, the idea doesn't

**3. Demo**
- Add two keyframes, create a push-in, play it back
- Show it with and without easing side by side **[CHECK: easing/curve controls in current CapCut PC build]**
- Keyframe opacity for a clean fade, keyframe position for a slide
- Combine two properties on one clip
- Show an over-animated version so they see the ceiling

**4. The Trap**
- Too much movement, too fast — motion that draws attention to itself instead of the subject
- Keyframing everything because you just learned how

**5. Drill**
- Add one push-in to one shot in your current edit
- Make it slow enough that a viewer wouldn't consciously notice it

## 🔴 LIVE EDIT 01 — Talking-Head Reel, Cuts Only
**Shot type:** Live Edit

**Format:** unscripted, real time, one raw take to a finished cut. No captions, no effects, no color, no music.

**What to hit:**
- Watch and mark the footage on camera before cutting
- Build the structure first — hook, tension, payoff — then refine
- Narrate the *decision* behind each cut, not the click
- Leave your mistakes in. Get one wrong, notice it, fix it out loud
- Talk through at least one moment where you removed something good because it didn't serve the video
- End by playing the result with nothing added — the whole point is that the cut already works

**Save this project. Live Edit 02 builds directly on it.**
