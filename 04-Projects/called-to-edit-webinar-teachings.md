### 0.4 The mindset: treat it as a business from day one; consistency over talent

Recorded 2026-10-19. **"Consistency over talent" dropped:** *"I don't think we need to be talking about consistency beats talent."* His own story of when he started treating it as a business is skipped too: *"I don't want to focus on me."* Working title from what he said: **the mindset: editing is a service, run it as a business.** He called these notes scattered, to be organised later: *"just take some of the things I'm saying and just put them down, then we can organize everything eventually."*

**Points**
- **Video editing is a service, not just a skill.** *"You learn the skill of the video editing, you sell the service."* You need an offer for a client or a business.
- **Business is an exchange of value for money.** *"I serve you by editing your videos and you give me cash for it. That's business."*
- **Where editors get stuck:** they learn the skill, leave it as a skill, and never package it as a service or convince the client why they need it.
- **In practice:** ask how you can offer this and who you can offer it to. Have an ideal client. Not everyone is your client.
- **The clothing example:** a clothing business can't dress people in Japan and China at once because they dress differently. You pick one demographic: *"I'm sewing clothes for Nigerians."*
- **Your target audience decides how you pitch.**
- **Serving a client changes how you learn:** you improve your skill based on what suits your target audience, not what you'd like to learn.
- **Many editors didn't make it** because they learned a skill but never turned it into a service for a target audience. They want to edit for everyone. *"If you are targeting everyone, you have no one."* Beginners think "I'm just starting, I want as much as I can get"; you can't edit for everyone, and you need to start somewhere.
- **Your tools decide who you can serve right now.** You can't edit on a phone and serve someone who needs YouTube videos. They may be your dream client, but your tools don't get you there yet.
- **Talented editors miss this** because they focus on the skill, not on how to serve the client.
- **Tools + skill + target audience decide what you serve.** Serve everyone and you end up like every other editor. Be specialised, not generalised. (Niching down comes later: section 3.)
- **Work backwards:** start from the tool you have (*"my phone, my computer, CapCut"*) and the person you want to serve (*"YouTubers or Instagram content creators or real estate agents"*). Those two decide what skill you go and learn. Then package that skill as a service for that audience.

**Teaching:** you learn a skill but you sell a service, to a specific client. Work backwards from your tools and who you want to serve to what you learn.

**Examples:** the clothing business (Japan vs China; *"I'm sewing clothes for Nigerians"*) · he wanted to be a motion designer, but his laptop was very bad and he didn't have the money · phone editor vs a client who needs YouTube videos.

## 1. Learning the skill

### 1.1 What to learn first when you don't know where to start

Recorded 2026-10-19. He asked for this one as lists, so it has structure: *"I need a lot of lists so that I can be able to have a structure and not just be rambling around."* The final order gets set on the whiteboard.

**The core idea:** *"You don't choose what to learn. What you learn is chosen for you"* by a few factors. You work backwards from them and *"eliminate what you don't need till you finally know what you need to learn."*

**The factors that choose what you learn**
1. **Who you want to work for, and what kind of videos.** Be very specific.
   - On YouTube: vlogs, business talking heads, reaction, gaming, podcast, storytelling, cinematic.
   - On Instagram: reaction, talking head, storytelling.
   - Adverts are a category of their own.
   - Platforms edit differently: *"YouTube talking head and Instagram talking head. They are not the same thing."* TikTok is different again, and so is LinkedIn. Short form vs long form.
2. **Who can actually pay you.** Business videos pay more than entertainment. Paying niches he named: AI and SaaS (his), real estate (*"They have money"*), logistics, solar companies. *"You need to niche into something first before you even pick your skill."*
3. **What you have: your hardware.** Phone or laptop, and how strong it is.
4. **What you have: your software.** CapCut can't do 3D motion graphics, *"so why learn the skill?"*

**Each kind of video needs different skills**
- Real estate: a lot of tracking and animation.
- Business talking heads: no tracking or animation needed.
- Some storytelling videos: visual effects.
- Adverts: other skills, some need visual effects.
- Some videos don't even need sound effects.

**Where phone editors win and lose**
- Lose: long-form YouTube, motion graphics, heavy animation. *"You can't compete with a computer video editor."* Many phone editors add animations because everyone else does.
- Win: simple short form, **event recaps**, talking heads cut into shorts that just need captions, simple social-media-style videos with captions, overlays and screenshots. *"Most of the mobile video editors I see succeeding very well are those ones that do event recaps and simple social media styled videos."*
- *"Focus on where your power can handle."*

**Worked example: a phone editor doing podcast recaps**
- Learn: cutting, sound design, typography and captions.
- Skip: motion graphics, *"crazy storytelling"*.

**Then define yourself.** Don't just say "I'm a video editor": there are many (movie, TikTok, visual effects, podcast). His line: *"I am a long-form video editor that edits business videos for YouTubers."* Specialising is what makes you stand out. Every niche chosen this way has an audience. The next step after learning is finding those people.

**The foundations every paid edit needs:** cutting, pacing, audio, captions.

**Software vs skill: learn both, and they're different**
- **Master the software first**, the one you'll actually use. For CapCut, sit through the long 4–5 hour tutorials. *"This is a part that you can't skip."* He mastered DaVinci Resolve: *"There's nothing you can tell me about DaVinci Resolve that I don't know about."*
- **The skill** is what achieves the kind of video you chose: the art of cutting, pacing, sound.
- The mistake: learning the software and thinking that's editing, without knowing how to run the skill.
- Your tool stack grows as you go, e.g. CapCut plus Claude as an AI tool.

**AI as part of what you learn**
- AI is a tool in your stack, depending on the videos you edit.
- It can't do cinematic, heavily animated videos yet. It can handle some simple talking heads, and on a laptop it can cut your videos for you.
- He uses Claude (Claude Code) in his own workflow to edit parts of his videos.
- A friend works with a client whose short forms are all edited by AI.
- **AI as a pitch:** *"I use AI to enhance your videos faster so that you can put out content faster."* Some creators and business owners *"need speed more than even fancy stuff."*
- Use AI tools if you can afford them, then pitch them, and **charge the client for the cost of the AI tools** you use. *(Checked 2026-10-19: he meant "AI cost", not "course".)*

**Rule for beginners:** *"Don't learn what you are not going to be able to pitch out to your client."* Everything looks exciting. Motion graphics is fine to learn if it's your target audience, everything checks out and your tools can handle it.

**Then where to learn is simple: YouTube.** It has everything. The problem is trying to learn everything (more in 1.2).

**Teaching:** don't start with the skill. Start with who you serve, what they pay for and what your tools can do. Cut everything else, then learn the software and the few skills that are left.

**Examples:** he wanted to learn everything (a flip animation he saw) and it was overwhelming, until he chose YouTube long-form business talking heads · he loved motion graphics but his laptop couldn't handle it, so he dropped it · he mastered Resolve · he uses Claude in his edits · a friend's client gets all short forms edited by AI · phone editors winning on event recaps.

**Lines to say:**
- *"You don't choose what to learn. What you learn is chosen for you."*
- *"You need to niche into something first before you even pick your skill."*
- *"Don't just say I'm a video editor."*
- *"Focus on where your power can handle."*
- *"Don't learn what you are not going to be able to pitch out to your client."*

**Board** (his ask: lists for structure, built on the whiteboard): a funnel. Who you serve and what video → who pays → your hardware → your software → what you learn. The video-type list per platform. The phone editor's win/lose columns. Software + skill as two boxes. The podcast-recap example as a worked case.

### 1.2 Where to learn: tutorials

Recorded 2026-10-19. He'll visualise almost all of it on the whiteboard: *"I'm going to visualize almost all these things in one way or the other."*

**Where: YouTube, for the skill.** *"I didn't learn from any other place than YouTube."* *"There's nothing you want to learn that you won't learn there."* He never paid for a skills course.

**The problem isn't the source, it's the search**
- People think one video can teach them all of video editing. *"No, that's wrong. Video editing is broad."*
- What you search for comes from 1.1: who you serve, what kind of video, who pays more, your hardware, your software.
- Example: he wanted typography for his videos, so he searched "typography tutorial", learned it and implemented it.

**Build a learning list (the method)**
1. Name your video type, e.g. talking-head business videos for Instagram creators.
2. Ask any AI tool: *"What are the skills I need to learn to edit talking head videos for business creators on Instagram?"*
3. It returns a list, e.g. cutting, pacing, captions, a little typography, overlays and outlines, B-roll, sound design, music.
4. Learn them **one by one**: watch one tutorial for one skill.
5. **Implement it straight away** on one practice video: a full video from YouTube or anywhere, or *record yourself on your phone*. Learn cutting → cut your own video. Learn typography → add it to that same video. Then sound design, then a little motion graphics, all on the same video.
- *"Don't learn without implementation."*

**Learn as you work**
- Mid-edit and don't know a transition? Search YouTube for it, or describe the transition you saw to an AI and ask how to do it. Learn it, then implement it.
- With clients: when a client asks "can you do this?", he says yes, then learns it while doing that project.
- *"I actually learned on the go more than just watching tutorials."*
- *"You can't learn video editing without actually being in the videos, practicing it."*

**No single teacher:** he recommends no specific creator. He learned from many, each for what he needed at that moment, never one creator from beginning to end.

**Make tutorials your entertainment:** there was a time he opened YouTube and watched tutorials for fun. *"Let the tutorials become your entertainment."*

**What's a waste of time:** learning something you don't know you'll implement. Watching one whole full tutorial instead of learning skill by skill from your list.

**Free vs paid**
- *"Everything you can pay for in a course when it comes to the skill is on YouTube."* Don't pay for skills.
- What YouTube hardly teaches is **the business**: talking with clients, packaging yourself, presenting yourself, *"everything I'm teaching here"*. YouTube teaches *"fancy stuff that you don't need"*.
- He paid for a community to learn the business. He learned the business side from marketers, sales people and people who run ads, not from video editors.
- *"If you don't know what you want to learn, that's when you end up paying for courses. And most of the time, you will not implement them."*

**AI tools:** once you find AI tools that help your editing, learn each one the same way, individually. Describing what you saw to an AI is itself a way to learn.

**Teaching:** YouTube has every skill. Make a learning list for your kind of video, learn one skill at a time and use it on a real video straight away. Pay only to learn the business.

**Examples:** typography search · saying yes to a client, then learning it on the job · watching tutorials for fun · paying for a community for the business side, taught by marketers and sales people.

**Lines to say:**
- *"Video editing is broad."*
- *"Don't learn without implementation."*
- *"Let the tutorials become your entertainment."*
- *"Everything you can pay for in a course when it comes to the skill is on YouTube."*
- *"If you don't know what you want to learn, that's when you end up paying for courses."*

**Board:** the learning-list loop: AI prompt → skill list → watch one → implement on the same video → next skill. Free (skills, YouTube) vs paid (the business). His visuals to be set on the whiteboard.

**Overlaps to sort when organising:** practising on your own recorded video overlaps with 1.3 (recreate videos you like). Learning from a client job overlaps with 1.7 (working for free as learning).

### 1.3 How to learn: recreate videos you like instead of only watching

Recorded 2026-10-19.

**Recreating videos in your niche is one of the fastest ways to learn.** It gets easier once you know what kind of videos you edit (1.1), because you know where to look. *"One of the things I did a lot was recreate what people have already edited."*

**How (his podcast example)**
1. Pick your niche, e.g. podcast videos.
2. Find an edit you love, e.g. someone's short form cut from a podcast.
3. Find the raw podcast on YouTube and download it.
4. Mimic the same edit.
5. When you hit something you can't do (an animation, a visual, a 3D effect, the sound design), search YouTube for how to do that one thing, learn it, then put it in. *"There's a YouTube tutorial for everything you want to do. I promise you."*

**Where to get raw footage**
- *"YouTube is a mine of raw videos."* Many people post unedited videos.
- **Don't use the biggest creators:** their videos are already over-edited. Look for raw videos similar to your niche.
- If you can't find the raw, use another kind of video.
- Download with one of the many download tools. **He plans to show them how to download YouTube videos live.** *Which tool: not said yet.*

**Share it:** put the original and yours side by side on social media: *"I just recreated this edit. What do you guys think? Did I get it right?"* Lots of editors post recreations.

**Practice, not portfolio:** *"a recreated video might not really be going into your portfolio... it's mostly for practice."*

**Why it works**
- You're implementing what someone already did professionally, *"so that you can one day become like that person."*
- It sharpens your skills and challenges you. You've never done it before and can't ask the editor who did it, so you have to work out how.
- He did it with motion graphics and styles he admired.

**Teaching:** pick an edit you love in your niche, find raw footage and copy it shot for shot, learning each missing piece on YouTube as you hit it.

**Examples:** recreating podcast shorts from the raw podcast · recreating motion graphics and styles he admired.

**Lines to say:**
- *"There's a YouTube tutorial for everything you want to do. I promise you."*
- *"YouTube is a mine of raw videos."*
- *"Don't look for the biggest creators. Those guys have already over-edited their videos."*

**Board:** the 5-step recreate loop · original vs recreation side by side · live demo: downloading a YouTube video.

### 1.6 How long until you're good enough to charge

Recorded 2026-10-19.

### 2.4 The decisions an editor makes and what should drive them

Recorded 2026-10-19.

**The goal drives every decision.** Every video has a goal; know it.
- A business video's goal: *"convert viewers into clients."*
- You measure your result by whether the video achieved the reason the client posted it.
- **Views vs sales:** a fancy video can get lots of views and no sales. A simple, clear video for the target audience can bring lots of sales. *"Which is more beneficial for the client?"*
- If the client's goal is views, then hit that goal instead.
- Ask: what is my client trying to achieve? What is their target audience looking for? What's the best thing this video presents? Build the edit around that.

**Every element needs a function.** *"Every cut, every animation that you do needs to have a reason for it to be in the video."* Each one must do one of two things:
1. **help the viewer understand** the knowledge in the video, or
2. **help the viewer feel an emotion.**
- Sad music used to carry the emotion of a story works. Sad music *"plastered for no reason"* doesn't.
- A transition has to play a role in the story.
- Random B-roll *"just to make it look like it is being edited"* is wrong. B-roll is there to help the audience see what's being said.

**Show, don't tell.** *"Whenever the client is saying something and you can show that thing, show it."*
- For clients: whatever he explains, Samuel visualises, with motion graphics and explainers.
- **Real beats fake:** if he's talking about a car, show a real car, not a car icon. *"A real thing always beats a fake thing."*
- Talking about YouTube views? Show YouTube views. A meeting? Show B-roll of that meeting if you can, or visualise it another way.
- Some of his videos are about **80% showing what the client says** rather than the client's face, depending on the video type.

**How viewers take in a video**
- **What they hear:** the speaker's voice helps them follow; sound effects carry feelings and emotion.
- **What they see:** visuals must match what they hear. A car sound needs something car-related on screen.
- *He said "three major ways a viewer digests a video" and named hearing (voice, sound effects) and seeing. Check what the third is.*

**Niche and platform shape the choices**
- Master one niche and you learn *"the ingredients"* that make its videos get results for the client.
- Platform matters: TikTok is fast; Instagram and YouTube editing each differ. Hold the viewer's attention.

**Why this makes you worth more:** *"A client will prefer an editor that can do videos that produce more results than a video editor that is doing flashy stuff and the video is not performing."* That's what tells you what to add or cut.

**Teaching:** edit for the client's goal, not to look edited. Every cut, effect, sound and B-roll must help the viewer understand or feel something. Show, don't tell.

**Examples:** visualising his clients' explanations with motion graphics · real car vs car icon · videos 80% visuals, not face · sad music with a purpose vs plastered.

### 4.3 A personal brand as a video editor

**Why:** people need to know you're a genuine, real human. *"This is the AI age"*: people impersonate, copy others' posts and generate with ChatGPT.

**What to post: your life around video editing**, nothing else for now (no politics, no football: *"You can, but right now you are trying to build a reputation"*).
- a normal day: you working, you on a call with a client
- **your story.** His soldier-to-editor story *"always hits as bangers every single time."*
- struggles and failures, e.g. a client who bounced you
- wins: a new client, a new laptop
- challenges: *"Day one of trying to get my first client, day two, day three."*

**The 40 / 40 / 20 rule: three kinds of post**
| Type | Share | What | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Authentic** | 40% | your story, timeline pictures, behind the scenes, you working, wins and failures | builds emotion |
| **Authority** | 40% | educational posts, e.g. *"Video editors, here are 3 ways to get a client"*, or for your niche: *"Business owners, these are the mistakes you make in your videos: 1, 2, 3"* | proves you know what you're doing: *"You are not just a dummy"* |
| **Sales** | 20% | *"Hey, I'm a video editor and I'm looking for a new business client"*: introduce yourself, say what you want | pitches you |

**How often:** at least **2–3 posts a day.** He posted 3 a day for 60 days, and it built his engagement.

**Followers don't matter as much as you think:** *"I only had 700 followers on my Twitter, but that 700 followers got me the best clients I needed."*

**Teaching (section 4 so far):** you can't get hired if no one can see you. Pick one platform, build a profile that says who you help and shows your best work, and post 2–3 times a day: 40% authentic, 40% authority, 20% sales.

**Examples:** his mentor's "where can people find you?" · following ~50 editors to curate his feed · 3 posts a day for 60 days · 700 Twitter followers, best clients · the soldier story as his best post · TikTok bringing Nigerian DMs.

**Lines to say:**
- *"You can't succeed in this industry if you are hidden."*
- *"Showing your work is your leverage."*
- *"You want to build something around a niche, you need to surround yourself with that niche."*
- *"People trust who they can see."*

### 4.4 Portfolio: where to build it and how to present yourself

Recorded 2026-10-20 (4.4–4.7 in one voice note).

**Why:** your socials show your work, but *"a new client... cannot go through and start scrolling all your posts."* A link in your bio takes them to where all your work lives: an archive of your work and proof that you're someone they can work with.

**A website, not Google Drive**
- *"The best portfolio that you can ever get is a website."*
- *"I go against Google Drive because it is very bad."* Making the client click every file is friction: *"I'm not ready for this"*, and they go to another editor. On a website they just watch.
- It doesn't need to be flashy or built by a web designer. *"What the client wants to see is your videos, not a flashy website."*

**Build it with AI (his method)**
1. Upload your edits to YouTube as unlisted ("hidden") videos.
2. Copy the links.
3. Paste them into an AI: *"These are the videos I edited. Use these videos to create a portfolio that I can link in my bio."*
- Claude does it very well (he recently built one for someone on Claude); ChatGPT and Gemini can too. Free versions: *"I believe they should be able to do something good."*
- His own: built on **Framer** in one night, using code and AI. *"I did that once and it got me clients."*
- **Live in class:** he'll show his old portfolio from when he was pitching himself.

## 5. Getting clients

### 5.1 Inbound: getting clients to come to you

Recorded 2026-10-20.

**Opening for section 5: skip the groundwork and this gets hard.** If you skipped being specific (your niche, who you serve) or being visible, *"getting clients will be difficult for you."* Establish yourself in one niche and be *"visible and loud about it."*

**Two ways to get clients**
| | What | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Inbound** | the client comes to you: an *"inbound lead"* | *"Hey, I saw your work, I would love to know how much you charge"* |
| **Outbound / outreach** | you approach the client | you see someone whose videos you could edit and reach out |
- **Do both.** *"You can't rely on only inbound and you can't rely on only outbound."* Established editors get more inbound; beginners have to go looking.

**What makes a client DM you rather than another editor**
- **Specificity**: your personal brand makes you unique.
- **Reliability.** Clients want editors who are reliable, diligent, hardworking and deliver on time. *"Clients are tired of editors that know how to do flashy edits, but they are not reliable"* and miss deadlines.
- **So document your day.** A client scrolls your profile, sees your day-to-day and thinks *"this guy is hardworking."* His time-lapses of himself working got that comment: *"this guy is locked in."*
- *"People are not just looking at the fancy videos. They want to see... the behind the scenes."* That attracts quality clients.

**Every view counts:** *"Those 200 views, one of them could be your client."* Or one of them knows your client and recommends you. Many people recommended him to clients because they followed him on Twitter.

**Trust:** *"I can't give you my money if I don't trust you."* Your content builds that reputation.

**What makes clients DM you, as a list:** showing your work · how you came up with the work · the person and personality behind it · your credibility: testimonials and the results the work brought · posting consistently (not once a week, not once every 2–3 days).

**Be patient:** 1, 2, 3 months of consistency. Once other editors comment, follow and like your posts, clients follow, *"because if you are valuable to other video editors, it means that you are valuable."*

**Teaching:** inbound only comes from work on your personal brand. Show your work, the process and you, every day, and be patient for 1–3 months.

**Lines to say:**
- *"Clients are tired of editors that know how to do flashy edits, but they are not reliable."*
- *"Those 200 views, one of them could be your client."*
- *"I can't give you my money if I don't trust you."*
- *"There are a lot of clients looking for people. You are invisible. That's why they can't find you."*

**Board:** inbound vs outbound · **his Twitter profile and posts that brought inbound leads, shown live** (*"you guys can see how much I was posting"*) · his time-lapse work videos.
