# Called to Edit webinar: the teachings

Part of [[04-Projects/called-to-edit-webinar-and-academy|Called to Edit webinar → Academy]]. Area: [[03-Areas/personal-brand/personal-brand|Personal brand]]. Topic list: [[04-Projects/called-to-edit-webinar-content|Webinar content]] (48 teaching topics after his cuts of 2026-10-04; Q&A not counted).

Samuel, 2026-10-16: *"you would mention each topic one by one and I would do a voice record here of what I would be talking about so we have an understanding of points, teachings and examples I would cover to be used eventually in the whiteboard or the slides"*.

**How each topic is filled:** he records what he'd say; the Brand manager writes it up as **Points** (what he covers), **Teaching** (the one thing they leave with), **Examples** (his stories and proof), **Board / slide** (what goes on screen). Everything comes from his recording. Anything not said stays blank. A topic he skips is marked *skipped*.

Webinar: Fri 2026-10-23, 7:00pm WAT, 2½ hours. Visuals built Mon 2026-10-19 and Tue 2026-10-20.

**Taught on a Canva whiteboard, not slides** (Samuel, 2026-10-16): *"I don't want slide slides because slides are kind of boring. I want to be able to move the whiteboard"*. Each section is lines and connections that tell the story, plus lists, revealed gradually, like a Miro board. Some parts switch to his Chrome browser to show things live. **Order:** all topics get recorded first, then the board gets mapped topic by topic so it builds progressively. The *Board* lines below are notes for that pass, not the layout.

**Where the weight goes** (Samuel, 2026-10-19): most of the audience are beginners who haven't worked with clients yet. Their two big concerns, from what they told him, are **how they will learn** and **how they will get clients and get paid**. Those two get the most emphasis: sections 1 and 5, plus getting paid in 6. *"Those two things are the major two things I need to emphasize a lot in this webinar because that's what a lot of them pointed out."*

## 0. Before the pathway

### 0.1 His story: zero to paid by a client abroad, working from Abuja

Recorded 2026-10-16.

**Points**
- **2020, COVID, still a soldier.** Learned editing for himself: his Minecraft YouTube channel. Laptop, wanted free software, found DaVinci Resolve. *"If I knew CapCut then, I might have started with CapCut, but I kind of started with DaVinci Resolve and I'm glad I did."* Later edited for his church.
- **Decided to go professional around 2022–2023** (he wasn't sure of the year): *"What can I do better than just being a soldier?"* Looked at skills, picked editing because he already knew a little.
- **Offered to edit for his mentor for free** when the mentor opened a YouTube channel. He only knew how to cut. *"The best move I made."*
- **Learned by doing real work with stakes.** Tutorials watched to deliver a video that would go live, not for practice. The first project came back: *"I can't use this."* The third one finally got uploaded.
- Had spent a whole salary on a laptop beforehand. Decent but laggy; it was enough.
- Unpaid at first, then an allowance of NGN 25,000 a month for data and expenses, about 4 videos a month. Did it for **two years** because it was making him better.
- **Mentor's advice: put your work out there.** New Twitter account, posted his work, networked with editors on Twitter and LinkedIn. Seeing others' work showed him he had to step up. Locked in for six months: mastered Resolve, learned the business side.
- **Paid USD 60 from his salary for a community** that taught the business of editing, not just the skill. Learned that the career around the skill is what makes you succeed, and the psychology behind each edit choice.
- Asked editors questions, entered DMs, got rejected, kept learning.
- **First client came to him by DM:** he'd been watching Samuel's posts. USD 20 per short-form video, his first dollar payment; didn't know how to get dollars into naira, used a virtual card platform. Same client went to USD 30, then about USD 60.
- Worked days in the military, edited at night.
- **Quit the military to go full time.** *"One of the most risky challenges I ever did."* Had some savings and a place to stay in Abuja.
- First month in Abuja: USD 600, his biggest payout then. Second month: about USD 1,000.
- Took on three clients at once and nearly burnt out. Learned *more clients does not mean you are better*; went after better clients. An agency paid USD 250 per video.
- Kept posting. **Route Rise reached out** because they'd been watching his work. Through them: a YouTube channel with 1M subscribers and a billion-dollar company.
- About four to five years in. Lots of mistakes, trial and error. *"I wish I knew what I knew two years ago."* Now that he's worked with *"the bigger boys"*, he knows not everything matters, and tonight is the things that do.

**Teaching:** the skill gets you in; how you present it is why you get hired. *"There are up to millions of video editors all around the globe. Why you?"*

**Examples** (all his): the rejected first project for his mentor · NGN 25,000 a month for 4 videos, for two years · the USD 60 community · the USD 20 first client who found him through his posts · USD 600 then USD 1,000 in his first months in Abuja · the three-client burnout · Route Rise finding him from his posts.

**Lines to say:**
- *"I didn't learn majorly by just tutorials for myself."*
- *"People watch what you do. People watch what you post."*
- *"Video editing is not just a skill. It is a way of passing information and communicating."*
- *"A lot of people put a lot of effect without understanding what purpose it is actually doing in the video."*

**Board** (his ask: points, pictures and the journey as a timeline on the Canva whiteboard): one timeline, left to right, each stop a picture plus one line: 2020 Minecraft channel → free work for his mentor (rejected first edit) → new Twitter account, posting → USD 60 community → first USD 20 client → quits the military → USD 600 / USD 1,000 months → three clients, burnout → USD 250 agency → Route Rise → 1M-subscriber channel and a billion-dollar brand. End the timeline on *"Why you?"*, which bridges into tonight. Pictures already found for the montage (first chair after the USD 600, work area) are listed in [[03-Areas/personal-brand/soldier-to-editor-montage-2026-10-16|the montage note]]. He picks the rest.

### 0.2 What tonight is and isn't: not a tutorial, the pathway to earning

Recorded 2026-10-16.

**Points**
- **Tonight is the pathway to the career, not editing tutorials.**
- **Video editing is broad:** movie editors, podcast editors, short-form editors, motion graphic animators. His definition: *"Editing is literally cutting out what is not needed from the raw idea and presenting it in a way that the consumer can get it."* He can't teach every kind, but the pathway works whatever kind you do.
- **Step-by-step tutorials are on YouTube.** *"I learned 80 to 90% of what I know in video editing from YouTube"*, and he'll show how to learn from it properly.
- **The AI age:** *"A lot of skills that you learn tomorrow will become useless next tomorrow."* So you need to know what to learn, where to start and how to find the right tutorials. Typing "video editing tutorial" into YouTube may not give you what's useful for you.
- **What tonight covers** (his roadmap, in his order):
  1. Learning the skill: what to learn, where to start, how to pick the right tutorials.
  2. Tools and money: the right tool for your kind of editing; the hidden costs (*"video editing is not cheap"*); gadgets and the computer. For those who've already started: line up what you already have and use it right.
  3. Choosing a niche.
  4. Establishing yourself: *"How does the world know that you are a good video editor?"* Social media, platforms, personal brand, portfolio and the tools for it, presenting yourself, packaging the skill, showing your work.
  5. Getting clients, *"one major thing"*: outbound, inbound (getting clients to find you), cold DMs, pitching, following up.
  6. Pricing and getting paid: *"A lot of us price ourselves wrongly."*
  7. Networking with other editors, *"a huge part of the career"*.
  8. Growing your income at the end, for people already established.
- **A teaser for later in the webinar:** *"another thing I'm planning to do... stay tuned for that"*. This sets up the Academy offer.
- Close: *"I believe that you guys are ready for it too. Let us get into it."*

**Teaching:** tutorials teach the buttons, and they're free on YouTube. Tonight teaches the path, which works whatever kind of editing you do.

**Examples:** he learned 80–90% from YouTube · editors earning big on just a phone or CapCut while others use expensive tools.

**Lines to say:**
- *"Editing is literally cutting out what is not needed from the raw idea and presenting it in a way that the consumer can get it."*
- *"I can't teach you every aspect of video editing, but I can tell you the pathway."*
- *"I know video editors that are making huge with their phone."*

**Board:** a cluster of editing types (movie, podcast, short-form, motion graphics) all pointing at one path. Then the roadmap as a row of stops, the frame the rest of the board hangs off: Learn → Tools and money → Niche → Establish → Clients → Pricing → Network → Grow. A crossed-out "tutorials" box next to a YouTube logo with "80–90%". A closed box at the far end marked "later tonight" for the Academy teaser.

**Settled 2026-10-19:** section 7 stays but is *"not a major emphasis"*; what he'll talk about there is testimonials, turned into case studies. See section 7.

### 0.3 The truth about the market: demand for editors, why most beginners never earn

Recorded 2026-10-19, answering a question list. He skipped two questions (top beginner mistakes, good-but-broke vs paid editor): *"these are questions that I don't want you to be asking me right now in this stage"*.

**Points**
- **There is a huge demand for editors.** He wants this backed by **statistics, not "a lot"**: how many creators there are, how many people use the platforms (*"billions of users"*), how much content goes out, how much demand there is for editors. *Stats not gathered yet; each one needs a named source.*
- **Three kinds of creators:** entertainment, educative, and business people. **Most educative creators are business people**: they educate so you eventually buy their product. They put out a lot of content, it's tedious for them, so they need editors most, and **they pay the most** (*"Eventually, we'll touch that"*: the niche section). Entertainment creators are plenty too and need editors.
- **Every business needs content now:** *"you can't run a proper business if you don't... put something out there on the internet."* Many of these people are busy, and they'll pay well for editing.
- **AI:** yes, AI can edit, but *"people don't even want to spend time asking AI to edit their videos."* The leverage is the skill **plus AI in your workflow**, so you deliver faster and easier. His company demands that its professional editors integrate AI. Clients want editors who are up to date, and creativity is still needed. *"AI is a very huge aspect of what I'm going to be talking about."* He'll show how to integrate it later.
- **Who pays: businesses and creators, and agencies.**
  - Agencies are middlemen. One may have 20–30 clients and employ many editors.
  - Good: they always have work, and give you access to clients you couldn't reach yourself. Working for agencies at one point *"really helped my career"*. **Good for beginners.**
  - Bad: they pay less because they take a cut, and they give the worst feedback and many revisions. Frustrating and painful. Working with a client directly has less of that.
  - **Best: creators, businesses and brands directly.**
- **The ladder:** big brands and big creators go to agencies; they hardly work with individual editors. Those who can't afford agencies are who individual editors work with. *"You kind of have to work your way up the ladder."*
- **"The market is full"? No.** *"Some creators have three, four, five video editors."* The problem is that most of us are bad editors and don't position ourselves so these people can find us. That's what tonight covers.
- **The main beginner mistake: learning a lot of skill but not positioning yourself.** Not using social media to get clients; putting random things in a portfolio and applying for jobs. Applying has its place, but only once you have a strong social media presence and portfolio. Before that, *"social media is where you build"* (covered properly in section 4).
- **His own main mistake:** not putting himself out there (more later).

**Teaching:** there's huge demand and a real chance for you, but you have to position yourself. *"A diamond is as valuable as it is visible."*

**Examples** (his): his company demanding AI from its professional editors · agencies helped his career · some creators have 3–5 editors.

**Lines to say:**
- *"A diamond is as valuable as it is visible."*
- *"You can't be a video editor without being visible because video editing is literally a visible kind of skill."*
- *"Our muscle is in the beauty of the video."*
- *"Some creators have three, four, five video editors."*
- *"You kind of have to work your way up the ladder."*

**Board** (his ask): the statistics mapped out on the whiteboard: creators, users, content volume, demand for editors. The rest of the board for this topic gets looked at after all topics are recorded (*"we'll look through"*).

**Open:**
- **Statistics:** to be found, each with a source.

**Settled 2026-10-19:**
- **Agencies belong to getting clients** (*"agencies are part of clients"*); most reach out on Twitter and LinkedIn saying they need an editor. He left the exact spot to the Brand manager: the full take goes in **5.2**, with a pointer in 5.1. In 0.3 they're only named as one of the payers.
- **AI moves to the start, into learning** (*"at the beginning, not even at the ending, AI is going to be part of what we need to learn"*): **1.1** and **1.2**, where to learn it and which tutorials. 9.4 stays for using AI to take on more work.
