For my first two or so years, nobody taught me the importance of visibility. Editing is a visible skill. To prove you're credible, you have to show your work. That's your selling point. That's your marketing. That's your advert.

Where can people find you? When they need you, where can they see your work?
The question my mentor asked me

I had nowhere to show it. So I opened brand-new Twitter, LinkedIn and Instagram accounts and did two things.

1
I curated my feed
I searched “video editors” and followed every major editor I found: about 50 on Twitter, and many on LinkedIn and Instagram. My feed fed me editing all day.
2
I posted my work
I recorded myself working, photographed my timeline, screenshotted everything I did in a day, and posted no less than 3 times a day.

If you want to build something around a niche, surround yourself with that niche. For your first month, lock in: make all your social media about editing, especially your niche. It's a secret most people don't know.

That visibility is what actually made me successful. No matter how good you are, if no one knows, it doesn't count. When people think of your name, they should think of what you do. Announce it and be unashamed.

Showing your work is your leverage.

@@OP|9|Get clients|Skill, a strong personal brand, a strong portfolio, outreach and a network of editors. Done together, consistently.|Getting clients is a lot of hard work. That's the truth.@@

If you skipped being specific about your niche, or skipped being visible, getting clients will be hard. The people you reach out to will check your page before they reply. If your page is empty, they won't even reply.

Inbound
They come to you
“Hey, I saw your work. How much do you charge?”
Outbound
You go to them
You see someone whose videos you could edit, and you reach out.

Do both. You can't rely only on inbound, and you can't rely only on outbound. Established editors get more inbound; beginners have to go looking.

{MK}Inbound: make them come to you

Inbound only comes from work on your personal brand. What makes a client DM you instead of another editor:

  • Showing your work, and how you came up with it.
  • The person behind it, your personality. Being specific makes you unique.
  • Reliability. Clients are tired of editors who can do flashy edits but aren't reliable and miss deadlines. So document your day. People don't only look at the fancy videos; they want the behind the scenes.
  • Credibility: testimonials and the results your work brought.
  • Posting consistently, not once a week, not once every two or three days.
{MKG}From my journey

I used to post time-lapses of myself working. A client scrolled my profile, saw my day-to-day, and said exactly this: “This guy is locked in.” That's what reliability looks like from the outside.

I can't give you my money if I don't trust you, and your content builds that trust. Be patient: one, two, three months of consistency. Once other editors comment on, follow and like your posts, clients follow, because if you are valuable to other editors, you are valuable.

{MKG}Remember

There are a lot of clients looking for editors. You are invisible. That's why they can't find you.

{MK}Outbound, part 1: warm outreach first

Warm outreach means people who already know you: friends, family, colleagues making content, people in your DMs and contacts, and other editors you know. Warm outreach converts the fastest, because they already know and trust you.

{MK}Outbound, part 2: find cold clients

Cold outreach is people who don't know you yet. It takes research and warming up. Go where your client is: for YouTube clients, search YouTube for creators in your niche. Watch their videos and understand what they do. Scroll past the many who already have great editors.

Find their email: on YouTube, many creators list one on their channel. Then find them everywhere else (Instagram, LinkedIn, Twitter), note the kind of content they make, and save examples to use as references. Even well-edited creators may need an extra editor.

{MK}The dream client list

This is the most important thing for every video editor. Make a table in Notion (I always recommend Notion) or a spreadsheet. You are gathering leads here, not reaching out yet.

  • Aim for 20 to 50 names. Mine was 100: my dream 100, built by hand before AI could research.
  • Keep adding every day until you land your first dream clients.
  • Keep two lists: potential clients, and clients you already work with.

{MK}Cold DMs: talk first, pitch later

Why most cold messages fail: “Hey, my name is Samuel, I'm a video editor, and I'd love to work for you. Here's my portfolio.” You are too cold. You just want their money, and you show no interest in what they do.

Research the client properly first, and leave two or three genuinely good comments. Take your time: it can make or break your career.

An example of how the conversation can go. Even when they don't need you, they reply more politely (“Right now I don't have the money for an editor”), and that leaves you room to follow up.

  • A question lowers their guard. There are a lot of scammers out there; a curious, casual person is not one.
  • They decide whether to post the free edit. You need to learn to give value.
  • In DMs, send voice notes. People want to hear real human beings. Others paste AI-written messages; your voice makes you stand out. Send your picture and your portfolio too.

{MK}Cold emails: be specific in one message

An email isn't a conversation, so prove you watched their video.

To: Joshua
Subject: Your video on 5 ways to get healthy
Good day, Joshua. I just saw your YouTube video about 5 ways to get healthy, and I love it. I noticed it isn't performing well. Do you have an editor? I feel your videos can perform better in this way, that way and this way. I would love to do a free edit for you. Let me know.

Name the video. Not “I saw your videos” but “your last video about 5 ways to get healthy.” Better still, mention something from 10 minutes in, so they know you watched it. The more research you do on the person, the better the replies.

{MK}Value first: edit their video before they ask

Don't wait to be offered a free trial. Download one of their videos, re-edit it and send it. They see you've already put in work.

  • If you edit a full minute in the name of value first, you're wasting your time: you have other people to reach, and this one may say no.
  • Pick a video you can visibly improve. Don't take a well-edited video and add things that don't make sense.
  • Email it: “I did a free edit for you. Check it out.” Also post it on Twitter and tag the creator. They may miss the email and see the post. Other creators see it too, and sometimes someone else wants that edit for themselves.
  • It gets attention and it's good practice. But it takes time, your edit has to be really good, and it sells how fancy your edit is, not everything else you bring, like making videos that convert.

{MK}Loom: pitch yourself on video

Subject: I recorded a video to explain some things for you

  • Loom lets you record a short video of yourself and send it as a link. I pitched myself on camera instead of in text. They see a real person who took the trouble; most people now use AI to automate emails. People relate to videos.

Don't give up because one message got no answer. By the third, try: “I realise you must be busy.
