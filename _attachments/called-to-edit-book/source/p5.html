{MK}What moves your price

There is no fixed price. Think of it like a mixing desk: every fader moves the final number.

  • Your skill and confidence.
  • The client's business, and how much the video matters to it. Some videos are very important to them and need a lot of work.
  • What the video is for. A paid advert is directly tied to money. Business videos: always charge more.
  • The work inside it: animation, motion graphics, B-roll, research. For documentaries, look at what the video actually needs.
  • The niche and the length.
  • Revisions. Set them in the deal, for example “a video with 3 rounds of revisions.”
  • The result you get them: the sales the video should bring.

{MK}Take control of the price

  1. Ask questions first: “Do you want motion graphics? What style of video? What do you want to achieve with this?” They see you know what you're doing.
  2. When they say it's too much, explain why: “There's motion graphics in this,” or “This video has to reach an audience that can buy your product, so I'm doing more research.”
  3. Price on a call (Chapter 9), so you can negotiate properly.

Clients who demand your price in the DMs get angry when you hold it back. They're comparing you with other editors and looking for the cheapest. You don't want to stoop that low. If you've tried everything and they flatly refuse a call, give them your flat price and let them decide.

{MKG}Remember

Don't be afraid of no's. Don't be afraid of rejection. It's part of the journey.

{MK}Stop collecting single projects

Pitch several videos at once. It's easier to sell a full package than one video after which they have to come back to you.

  • Mine now: 3 to 6 videos a month for my client, paid at the end of the month for the videos done.
  • Packages need a retainer client: someone who already posts consistently and works with you over a long time, so you know the money comes back every month.

{MK}Don't get scammed

The scam is simple: you deliver the video and never get paid. It happens a lot in this industry.

  1. Take about 30% upfront before you start, especially from people you don't trust yet.
  2. On a new bundle, always. If you agree 20 or 30 videos a month with a new client, don't wait until all 30 are done for the first payment.
  3. Write a contract and have them sign it. Not something they just said or typed in a chat. You each keep a signed copy.

{MK}Getting paid from abroad

What works depends on where you live and where your client is. US clients pay differently from Canadian ones. Research what works between your country and your client's, and ask people near you who already earn online. What works for me might not work for you.

In Nigeria, receiving payment from abroad has been very difficult. I've used Payoneer, then Grey, and for the past two years Cleva. I've used Western Union before, but I don't think it works for this anymore. Here is what I found, as of October 2026 (fees change, so check inside the app):

Cleva (mine)
A US dollar account; convert to naira. Small deposit fee, no conversion fee reported.
Grey
USD, GBP and EUR accounts. Good for UK and European clients.
Raenest
A USD account built for freelancers. Fees vary; check in the app.
Payoneer
For Fiverr, Upwork and clients who already use it. Usually the dearest route into naira.
PayPal
Not a way to get paid in Nigeria: accounts there are mostly for paying, not receiving.
@@OP|11|Grow through people|Your biggest clients come through trust: results you can show, and people who know you.|You can't do video editing alone.@@

One of the most important assets you have is real testimonials and real results from your previous clients. Clients love to work with people who are already experienced and have worked with others.

  1. Document the result for every client, for example the views their videos got.
  2. Keep screenshots of those results.
  3. Turn them into case studies.
  4. Ask for a testimonial: written, where they type out what you did for them, or video, where they record themselves saying it. Video is very powerful.

Some of us have worked with people and never asked them for a testimonial. Go and ask.

{MKG}Do this

Ask one past client, or anyone you have edited for, for a testimonial this week.

{MK}Network with other editors

Other editors are very important to your growth. This helped me a lot.

When I started on Twitter, I engaged other editors' posts: I commented, liked, talked with them and built relationships. I joined their challenges, like “10 days to get my first client”, and cheered them on. They engaged my posts back. In DMs we looked at each other's edits and corrected each other. Some became friends who sent me client projects and opportunities.

  • Social media is meant to be social. Build a community of editors like you.
  • Where: LinkedIn and Twitter are the best I've seen; Instagram works too.
  • Who: editors at your level or smaller. Let them know you and engage with them.
  • Keep your feed full of editing and editors (Chapter 8). Editors you know are also warm outreach: offer to edit for them.

{MK}Join a serious community

The pathway in twelve lines

  1. Editing is a service, not just a skill. Sell it to a specific person.
  2. Niche first: business, SaaS, AI and real estate pay; entertainment mostly doesn't.
  3. Your tools decide what you can serve right now. Start with what you have; never cracked software.
  4. What you learn is chosen for you. The software first, then the few skills your niche needs, one at a time, on a real video.

  6. Three full videos in your niche and you're ready to charge.
  7. Free work with a set scope, then low pay, then paid. Say you're building your portfolio.
