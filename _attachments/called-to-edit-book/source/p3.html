@@OP|6|Learn the skill the right way|Start with who you serve, what they pay for and what your tools can do. Then learn one skill at a time, on a real video.|You don't choose what to learn. What you learn is chosen for you.@@

You don't choose what to learn. What you learn is chosen for you by a few filters. Work backwards through them and cut what you don't need, until you know exactly what to learn.

  1. Who you want to work for, and what kind of videos. Be very specific. Platforms edit differently: a YouTube talking head and an Instagram talking head are not the same thing. TikTok is different again, and so is LinkedIn. Short form is not long form.
  2. Who can actually pay you. Business videos pay more than entertainment (Chapter 4).
  3. Your hardware: phone or laptop, and how strong it is.
  4. Your software: CapCut can't do 3D motion graphics, so why learn that skill?

{MK}The four foundations

Every paid edit, in every niche, stands on four things.

{MK}Software and skill are two different things

The software
Master it first
The one you will actually use. For CapCut, sit through the long 4 to 5 hour tutorials. You can't skip this part.
The skill
Then learn the craft
The art of cutting, pacing and sound that makes the kind of video you chose.

The mistake is learning the software and thinking that's editing. I mastered DaVinci Resolve; there's nothing you can tell me about Resolve that I don't know. But the software was only the start. Your tool stack grows as you go, for example CapCut plus an AI tool.

{MK}AI is part of what you learn

  • AI is a tool in your stack, depending on the videos you edit.
  • It can't do cinematic, heavily animated videos yet. It can handle some simple talking heads and even some kinds of motion graphics, and on a laptop it can cut your videos for you.
  • I use Claude (Claude Code) in my own workflow to edit parts of my videos. A friend of mine works with a client whose short forms are all edited by AI.
  • If you can afford AI tools, use them, pitch them, and charge the client for the cost of the AI tools you use on their work.
  • Learn each AI tool the same way you learn editing: one at a time.
{MKG}AI is also a pitch

“I use AI to enhance your videos faster, so you can put out content faster.”

Some creators and business owners need speed more than fancy edits.

{MK}Where to learn: YouTube

I didn't learn the skill anywhere other than YouTube. There is nothing you want to learn that you won't find there, and I never paid for a skills course. The problem is never the source. It's the search. People think one video can teach them all of video editing. It can't: video editing is broad. What you search for comes from your filters.

  1. Name your video type, for example talking-head business videos for Instagram creators.
  2. Ask any AI tool: “What are the skills I need to learn to edit talking-head videos for business creators on Instagram?” It gives you a list: cutting, pacing, captions, a little typography, overlays and outlines, B-roll, sound design, music.
  3. Learn them one by one. One tutorial for one skill.
  4. Use it straight away on one practice video: a full video from YouTube, or one you record of yourself on your phone. Learn cutting, cut that video. Learn typography, add it to the same video. Then sound design, then a little motion graphics, all on the same video.
{MKG}Remember

Don't learn without implementation.

  • Learn as you work. In the middle of an edit and don't know a transition? Search YouTube for it, or describe the transition you saw to an AI and ask how to do it. When a client asks “Can you do this?”, I say yes, then learn it while doing that project. I learned on the go more than from watching tutorials.
  • No single teacher. I learned from many creators, each for what I needed at that moment, never one creator from beginning to end.
  • Make tutorials your entertainment. There was a time I opened YouTube and watched tutorials for fun.
  • What wastes your time: learning something you won't implement, and watching one long general tutorial instead of learning skill by skill.

{MK}Recreate videos you love

Recreating edits in your niche is one of the fastest ways to learn, and I did it a lot.

  1. Pick your niche, for example podcast videos.
  2. Find an edit you love, for example a short form someone cut from a podcast.
  3. Find the raw podcast on YouTube and download it.
  4. Copy the same edit.
  5. When you hit something you can't do (an animation, a 3D effect, the sound design), search YouTube for that one thing, learn it, then put it in. There's a YouTube tutorial for everything you want to do. I promise you.
  • Raw footage: YouTube is a mine of raw videos; many people post unedited ones. Skip the biggest creators: their videos are already over-edited. Look for raw videos close to your niche.
  • Share it side by side with the original. Lots of editors post recreations.
  • It's practice, not portfolio. You can't really call a recreation your own.
  • Why it works: you're copying what someone already did professionally, so that one day you become like that person. You can't ask the editor how, so you have to work it out.

{MK}Edit for the goal, not to look edited

Every video has a goal. Know it. A business video's goal is to turn viewers into clients. You measure your work by whether the video achieved the reason the client posted it.

The flashy video
Lots of views
No sales
Views the client can't use for anything.
The clear video
Fewer views
Lots of sales
Made for the right audience. Which would your client rather have?

Ask: what is my client trying to achieve? What is their audience looking for? What's the best thing this video presents? Build the edit around that. If the client's goal really is views, hit that.

Sad music that carries the emotion of a story works. Sad music plastered on for no reason doesn't. A transition has to play a role in the story. B-roll is there to help the audience see what is being said, not to make the video look edited.

{MK}Show, don't tell

Whenever the client says something you can show, show it.

Weaker
A car icon
The viewer reads a symbol.
Stronger
● REC
Real car footage
The viewer sees the real thing.

A real thing always beats a fake thing. Talking about YouTube views? Show YouTube views. A meeting? Show the meeting. For my clients, whatever they explain, I visualise, with motion graphics and explainers. Some of my videos are about 80% showing what the client says rather than the client's face.

What viewers hear and see must match. The voice helps them follow; sound effects carry feeling; the visuals show what is being said. A car sound needs something car-related on screen.

Master one niche and you learn the ingredients that make its videos get results. Platform matters too: TikTok is fast, and Instagram and YouTube each edit differently. Hold the viewer's attention.

{MKG}Remember

A client will choose an editor whose videos produce results over one doing flashy edits that don't perform.

@@OP|7|From practice to paid|Finish three full videos in your niche. Then get onto real people's videos: free at first, then a token, then paid.|When you can finish a full video from beginning to end, you're good enough to charge.@@

There's no fixed time before you're ready. It's about your skill level. When you can finish a full video from beginning to end, the kind you want to be paid for, you're good enough to charge. Not one: three.

Most people wait too long because they never finish a whole project. They learn nuggets and specific styles, not full projects, so they don't know how to put it all together on a real video. Finish one, two, three full projects and you build the confidence to take on a real person's video.

{MK}The ladder from practice to paid

  1. Practice videos: your own, like two or three talking heads of yourself.
  2. Real people's videos, for free. As soon as possible, move from practice to real projects:
    • a friend who owns a business: “Record a video for your business, let me help you edit it so you can post it.”
    • an event you attend: capture it, edit it, show them so they can use it
    • small businesses near you without the budget for an editor
    • small creators, or a friend making content in the style you want to edit
  3. Ask for a token. If they're enjoying it, tell them you'd love to be paid a token for it.
  4. Charge.

Offer free help, but still put your work out there as a paid service. If someone sees your practice videos and asks “How much do you charge?”, don't say “I'm not ready.”

{MK}Why real projects teach you fastest

My number one way to learn is working on real projects that have real consequences, for real people. The reason is pressure.

Your practice video
No deadline
You can spend a whole month on one video. Nothing is at stake.
A real project
They post it tomorrow
You try your best not to make mistakes, so it converts and gets views. It keeps you up late.

To get real projects as a beginner, offer your service free to people who can manage you as a beginner, where excellence isn't critical yet.

{MK}Work for free without getting stuck there

  1. Free is the best way to start.
  2. Low pay comes next, once you're more confident and have done real work, even a small amount (in Nigeria, NGN 5,000 or 10,000: a few dollars). It still makes you better, and it pays for what learning costs: data and electricity. Free or not, you still need data.
  3. Don't stay free too long. After four or five free projects for one person, start moving to paid, unless you have an agreement. Many of us spend too much time learning and working for free, and that's why we can't afford the little things we need.

You hear editors say they earn big monthly figures. You are not there yet. You are still in the learning stage, and at that stage you need enough money to fund your skill.

  • Set the scope up front. Never “free for a year”.
  • Don't tell them you're learning. Tell them you're building your portfolio.
  • You don't have to raise the money conversation. With a scope set, when it ends they know you either leave or they pay you. If you did a good job, they know they may not find someone as good, and they will want to pay you to stay. Then you agree a new deal. If they won't pay, you can leave for another client.
