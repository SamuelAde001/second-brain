@@OP|3|Editing is a service|You learn a skill, but you sell a service, to a specific person. Everything after this chapter rests on that.|If you are targeting everyone, you have no one.@@

Business is an exchange of value for money, literally. I serve you by editing your videos and you give me cash for it. That's business.

A skill
“I can edit videos.”
True of millions of people. Nobody knows what to buy from you.
A service
“I edit YouTube videos for business creators so they get more views.”
A specific person knows you are for them.

This is where most editors get stuck. They learn the skill, leave it as a skill, and never package it as a service or show a client why they need it. Talented editors miss this all the time, because they focus on the skill and not on how to serve the client.

So ask: how can I offer this, and who can I offer it to? You need an ideal client. Not everyone is your client.

{MK}Not everyone is your client

Think about a clothing business. It can't dress people in Japan and China at once, because they dress differently. So it picks one group.

Editing works the same way. Your target audience decides how you pitch, and it decides what you learn: you improve your skill based on what suits the people you serve, not on what you would like to learn.

Many editors never made it because they wanted to edit for everyone. As a beginner you think, I'm just starting, I'll take as much as I can get. But you can't edit for everyone, and you need to start somewhere. Serve everyone and you end up like every other editor. Be specialised, not generalised.

{MK}Your tools decide who you can serve right now

If you edit on a phone, you can't serve someone who needs long YouTube videos. They may be your dream client, but your tools don't get you there yet.

{MKG}From my journey

I wanted to be a motion designer. But my laptop was very bad and I didn't have the money for a better one. So I didn't chase it. I chose what my tools could do, and that is what got me paid.

{MK}Work backwards

Don't start with the skill. Start from two things you already know: the tools you have (your phone, your computer, CapCut) and the person you want to serve (YouTubers, Instagram creators, real estate agents). Those two decide what you go and learn. Then you package that skill as a service for that person.

{MKG}Remember

Video editing is not just a skill, it's a service. Your tools, your skill and your target audience decide what you can serve.

@@OP|4|Choose your niche|Pick a niche you enjoy, one that makes money and values marketing. Then say it in one sentence.|In any niche, business people pay more.@@

You need to niche into something before you even pick your skill. The niche decides who pays you, how much, and what you need to learn.

{MK}Who pays: my ranking

{MK}How to choose yours

  1. What excites you most, and what do you watch most? I watch a lot of business videos, so I understand how they are edited.

{MK}Short form or long form?

Long form
$500–2,000
per video is a price you easily hear in long form. It pays more per video.
Short form
5–10
videos a month in one package. Less per video, so you sell volume.

Ads are the best-paying short form. An ad is tied directly to the customers a business gets, so it is easy to measure how your video performs. I know editors who edit ads, and they make a lot of money. A short-form editor can earn more than a long-form editor if they know how to package their work well (Chapter 10).

{MK}Say it in one sentence: your offer

Stop saying “I'm a video editor.” There are too many kinds. Say exactly what you are: “I am a long-form video editor who edits business videos for YouTubers.” Then turn it into an offer. There must be a promise.

My example: “I help business creators get 10,000 views in 3 months through video editing and visually engaging content.” Always make sure you are specific.

{MKG}Do this

Write down what editing service you offer, to whom (the person you would love to work with), which kind of videos, and how you will package and sell it to them. Then write your offer sentence.

@@OP|5|Your tools and the money to start|Start on what you can afford, never on cracked software, and treat every cost as capital you will earn back.|Whatever you pay for a subscription is your capital.@@

Your niche and your hardware pick your software. If you don't have the hardware for a piece of software, don't chase videos that need skills only that software gives you.

On a phone
CapCut, Premiere Pro mobile, Edits
Free to start, with paid tiers. Great for short form, captions and simple social videos.
On a laptop
DaVinci Resolve, Premiere Pro + After Effects, CapCut Desktop, Final Cut (Mac)
Resolve is free, Premiere is paid. Needed for long form, colour and motion.
  • DaVinci Resolve: a great all-rounder, especially for colour grading. Free, but it needs a laptop that can run it. Colour grading is part of editing, and some people sell colour grading alone.
  • Premiere Pro + After Effects: the best for heavy motion design. Resolve can do it, but these work best. Paid.
  • CapCut: the free version is good and enough to get you paid work, but most professional tools need Pro. If you can afford a laptop, start with CapCut on the laptop, depending on your niche.
  • Edits (phone): I use it, especially for Instagram-style music.

You can't compete with a computer editor on heavy work. Many phone editors add animations because everyone else does. The phone editors I see succeeding are the ones doing event recaps and simple social media videos. Focus on where your power can handle.

{MK}Invest in your software

{MKG}From my journey

I once used a cracked copy of Premiere Pro while trying to move away from Resolve. I'm not joking: my computer was hacked, and it affected my client work. I went straight back to Resolve. Never use cracked software.

  • Phone editors: pay for CapCut Pro. If you don't have the money, borrow it for the first subscription, then work hard that month to earn the next one.
  • AI tools depend on your niche. I pay for Claude now, but a beginner shouldn't pay for a tool they don't need. Other paid tools exist, like ones that cut long form into short forms. If you need one, invest, then make a profit quickly to cover it.
{MKG}Remember

Whatever you pay for a subscription is your capital. Once you see it that way you think, “I'm already spending capital, I need to start making profit,” and that pushes you to pitch.

{MK}What editing really costs

Video editing is expensive. You can start for free, but it's not really free.

There is no fixed price to start, because everything varies by country and by what you already own. If you have a phone and can afford data, you can start. My first laptop cost a whole month's salary at the time. Later on, things change.

Treat your first one or two months as capital, an investment in your business and your skill. That is why you move from free to paid work fast (Chapter 7): paid clients cover the cost, then you break even, then you profit.

{MK}The computer you need

For DaVinci Resolve, the minimum is 16 GB of RAM, an SSD and a dedicated graphics card (GPU). These are expensive, so start with what you have and pick niches it can handle. Proper colour grading, for example, can't be done on a phone.

  • Don't wait for the perfect machine. Some people say they'll wait until they earn NGN 1 million before buying their first laptop. That will take a while. If all you can afford is an NGN 300,000 laptop (about USD 220), buy it and use it until you can move to an NGN 600,000 one (about USD 450).
  • Mac laptops (M1 and newer) are the best for editing. I couldn't afford one, so I used Windows. If you can, save up for one: it will make your life easier.
  • A bad laptop will frustrate you, but it can still edit some kinds of video.
{MKG}Remember

Don't spend your editing money on yourself yet. Putting my earnings back into my tools was one of the fastest ways I grew.
